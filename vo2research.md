## General Information

VO2 max is the single best lab measure of cardiorespiratory fitness and one of the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality. It is what your watch estimates when it shows a "fitness age" or cardio score. This guide breaks the factors that move VO2 max into categories: exercise/training, nutrition and supplements, environment, demographics and physiology, lifestyle, and clinical conditions. A separate section covers how consumer wearables estimate VO2 max and how accurate they are versus a lab test. As with the other library files, each row has a plain-English column and an evidence grade, and (with one noted exception) every claim is anchored to a primary source.

A note on what VO2 max actually responds to: training and biology dominate. Most supplements that get marketed for "endurance" do not raise VO2 max itself, they change the oxygen cost of a given effort (economy). We call that out explicitly below because it is a common misconception.

## Definitions

- **VO2 max:** The maximum rate your body can take in, transport, and use oxygen during all-out exercise. Usually expressed in mL/kg/min.
- **VO2 peak:** The highest VO2 actually reached in a test when a true plateau is not confirmed. Often used interchangeably with VO2 max in studies.
- **Oxygen cost / economy:** How much oxygen you burn at a fixed submaximal effort. Better economy means less O2 for the same work. This is separate from VO2 max.
- **Trainability:** How much your VO2 max improves in response to a training program. Varies hugely between people.
- **SMD:** Standardized mean difference, an effect size. Roughly: 0.2 small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large.
- **MICT:** Moderate-intensity continuous training (steady cardio). **HIIT:** High-intensity interval training.

## Exercise / Training

|---|---|---|---|---|
| HIIT (long interval, high volume) | Positive (strongest) | Strong | vs MICT, only long-interval (≥2 min), high-volume (≥15 min), 4 to 12 week HIIT beat steady cardio (SMD 0.65 to 1.07). Against no training, HIIT overall gave SMD 0.41 to 1.81, and this subgroup 0.50 to 2.48. 53 RCTs. | Hard intervals are the most effective way to raise VO2 max. The version that wins uses longer work bouts (2 min+) and enough total volume, run for at least a month. |

They are not clearly better than steady cardio though. |
| Moderate continuous cardio (MICT) | Positive | Strong | Reliable VO2 max gains across populations; only beaten by higher-volume HIIT. | Steady-state cardio works. It is just not as time-efficient as well-structured intervals. |
| Sprint interval training (SIT) | Positive | Moderate-to-Strong | Systematic review of 19 studies; 13 pooled for VO2 max: brief all-out sprints (e.g. repeated ~30 s efforts) over 2 to 8 weeks raised VO2 max versus control (Hedges' g 0.63, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.87), a +4.2 to +13.4% improvement in sedentary and recreationally active adults. | Very short, very hard sprint sessions reliably raise VO2 max, even with little total work. Great low-time option, but they are genuinely unpleasant. |
| Resistance training (alone) | Positive (modest, short programs) | Moderate | Meta-analysis in healthy adults over 60 (37 studies; 24 reported VO2 peak, 22 pooled): resistance training alone raised VO2 peak by 1.89 mL/kg/min (95% CI 1.21 to 2.57) in programs of 24 weeks or less; beyond 24 weeks the pooled effect was null (-0.01). No head-to-head comparison against aerobic training. | Lifting gives a modest VO2 max bump in healthy over-60s, concentrated in the first six months. This review did not compare it against cardio, so it cannot tell you which raises VO2 max more. |
| Detraining (stopping training) | Negative | Strong | Meta of 21 athlete studies: VO2 max fell after short-term (≤30 days) cessation (ES -0.62, avg -3.93%) and roughly twice as much after long-term (>30 days) cessation (ES -1.42, avg -9.43%). Higher-trained athletes lost more in the long-term analysis only. | Fitness is use-it-or-lose-it. A month off already costs around 4%, and longer layoffs cost more than twice that. Light activity during a layoff blunts the long-term loss. |
| Overtraining / non-functional overreaching | Negative | Moderate | ECSS/ACSM consensus: too much training with too little recovery causes prolonged performance decrements, including reduced VO2 max, that take weeks to months to resolve (distinct from short-term "functional" overreaching that rebounds). Scope note: a narrative expert consensus on overtraining syndrome as a whole, not a pooled VO2 max analysis. | More is not always better. Pushing past recovery can stall or drop your VO2 max. If your fitness is sliding despite hard training, you may be under-recovered, not under-trained. |
| Tapering (pre-event) | Positive (performance); no VO2 max outcome | Strong | Meta-analysis of 27 studies. Performance was maximized by a ~2-week taper (ES 0.59 ± 0.33) that exponentially cut training volume by 41 to 60% (ES 0.72 ± 0.36) while leaving intensity (ES 0.33 ± 0.14) and frequency (ES 0.35 ± 0.17) unchanged. VO2 max was not an outcome in this meta-analysis. | Backing off volume before a goal event sheds fatigue so your existing fitness shows up on the day. Every arm did both training types, so there is no endurance-only comparison and the review does not test whether adding strength work changes VO2 max gains. | The order you put lifting and cardio in does not change your VO2 max. Whether adding lifting to cardio helps or hurts is a different question this evidence does not answer. |

## Nutrition & Supplements

| Factor | Direction | Evidence | Key Finding | Plain English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron (when deficient) | Positive | Moderate-to-Strong | Meta of 24 RCTs in women of reproductive age, with the VO2 max estimate pooled from 18 of them: daily iron raised relative VO2 max by 2.35 mL/kg/min (95% CI 0.82 to 3.88, p=0.003) and lowered submaximal heart rate. Only 3 of the 24 trials were rated at low risk of bias. | Fixing low iron raises VO2 max, especially in menstruating women who are prone to deficiency. This is correcting a deficit, not a boost on top of normal iron. |

It makes a given pace cheaper to hold (better economy), which can help performance without changing VO2 max. |
| Caffeine | No reliable effect on VO2 max | Moderate | RCT (n=9): caffeine improved time to exhaustion (~+19%) and peak power (~+13%) but did not change VO2 max, and the gains were matched by a placebo believed to be caffeine. Broader reviews agree caffeine is ergogenic without raising VO2 max. | Caffeine helps you push harder and feel less effort, which improves performance. It does not raise your VO2 max ceiling. A lot of the benefit is expectation/placebo. |
| Creatine | Slightly negative (relative VO2 max) | Strong | Meta of 19 RCTs (n=424): VO2 max rose less with creatine than placebo (ES -0.32, 95% CI -0.51 to -0.12). Added body water/mass is the authors' *likely* explanation, not a measured mechanism. | Creatine is great for strength and power, and since VO2 max is scored per kg, the number can dip slightly. It does not improve aerobic capacity. |

The stand does not address VO2 max as an outcome — it is silent, not negative. | Useful for buffering "the burn" in efforts of roughly one to four minutes. There is no evidence it raises VO2 max, because that was never tested here. |

The review reports no VO2 max outcome. ## Environment

| Factor | Direction | Evidence | Key Finding | Plain English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Altitude (live high, train low) | Positive | Moderate | Elite orienteers living at 2,500 m (18 h/day) and training lower for 24 days raised hemoglobin mass (805 to 848 g) and VO2 max (3,515 to 3,660 mL/min). The unchanged control group were cross-country skiers, and their "sea level" was in fact 500 to 1,600 m. | Sleeping at altitude builds more oxygen-carrying red cells, which can lift VO2 max. Evidence is one small elite study whose controls were a different sport living at low altitude, and the effect is still debated. |
| Acute altitude (being at altitude) | Negative | Moderate | VO2 max falls roughly 6 to 8% per 1,000 m of elevation above about 1,500 m; roughly half recovers with ~2 weeks of acclimatization. Textbook figure from the altitude-physiology literature, not a specific cited study — treat the percentage as an approximation. | The thinner the air, the lower your VO2 max on the spot. Your watch will read lower at elevation until you adapt. |
| Heat acclimation | Positive | Moderate | Meta-analysis pooled four heat-acclimation effects on VO2 max: Hedges' g = 0.42 overall, 0.63 when tested in the heat, 0.30 versus a control group, and 0.75 versus control when tested in the heat. The +5% cool / +8% hot and ~6.5% plasma-volume figures come from Lorenzo 2010 (12 acclimated cyclists vs 8 controls, tested at 13°C and 38°C). | Training in the heat expands blood plasma and can nudge VO2 max up, similar in spirit to altitude. The boost is clearest when you are also tested in heat; transfer to cool-weather performance is less certain. |
| Air pollution (PM2.5) | Negative | Moderate | Systematic review of 24 studies: breathing polluted air during endurance exercise impairs cardiopulmonary responses and can blunt the aerobic-fitness benefit of training. A dose-response relationship between pollutant concentration and effect size was explicitly *not* established. (The "PM2.5 reaches the bloodstream" claim is not in this paper.) | Hard training in heavy traffic or smog works against you. How much depends on exposure in ways the evidence has not pinned down. Favor cleaner air and times of day when you can. |
| Hypoxic training methods | Positive (varies by method) | Moderate | Bayesian network meta-analysis (59 studies) ranked live-high/train-low combined with low-altitude training best (P-scores 0.92 and 0.86); intermittent hypoxic training ranked far lower (P-score 0.37) and intermittent hypoxic exposure at rest lowest (0.07). Source 52 (Dorelli 2025, 35 studies) directly contradicts this row for IHT: no VO2 max benefit over normoxic training (p=0.333), with publication bias detected. | Structured altitude/hypoxia protocols can raise VO2 max, but the method matters: sleeping high while training low is the most dependable. Intervals in a hypoxic mask is the weakest version, and the newest meta-analysis finds it does not work at all. |

*Anchors: Wehrlin et al. 2006 (live high, train low); Waldron et al. 2021 meta + Lorenzo et al. 2010 (heat acclimation); González-Rojas et al. 2025 systematic review (air pollution); Feng et al. 2023 network meta + Dorelli et al. 2025 meta (hypoxic training methods). Note: the "acute altitude ~6 to 8% per 1,000 m above ~1,500 m" row is a well-established textbook figure from the broader altitude-physiology literature surfaced during research, not a single numbered source above; graded Moderate for that reason and flagged for a dedicated citation in a future pass.*

## Demographics & Physiology

| Factor | Direction | Evidence | Key Finding | Plain English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Body fat / excess weight | Negative (relative VO2 max) | Strong | VO2 max is usually expressed per kg of body weight, so carrying more fat lowers the number. In adults with overweight/obesity, training raised relative VO2 max by 3.82 mL/kg/min (66 RCTs, n=3,964) — that is a *training* effect, not the effect of fat loss on its own. | Because the score is "per kilo," losing fat raises your VO2 max number even if your heart and lungs do not change. Training plus fat loss is the big lever here. |
| Age | Negative | Strong | In the Baltimore Longitudinal Study (n=810, ages 21 to 87), peak VO2 decline accelerates each decade rather than being linear: roughly 3 to 6% per decade through the 20s and 30s, rising to more than 20% per decade from the 70s onward. The decline is faster in men from the 40s onward. Cross-sectional studies underestimate the true rate. | VO2 max drops with age, and the drop speeds up sharply in later decades. Single snapshots across ages make aging look gentler than it really is. |
| Immobilization / bed rest | Negative (severe) | Moderate | Dallas Bed Rest Study (5 healthy men): 3 weeks of strict bed rest cut VO2 max ~26%, reversed by 8 weeks of training. The 40-year follow-up found 40 years of aging cost ~27% — a comparable drop. Five participants, no control group; the usual "falling cardiac output" mechanism is not established by this study. | Extended inactivity is brutally fast at erasing fitness. Three weeks flat on your back cost these men about as much VO2 max as four decades of aging — from a five-man study, so hold the exact number loosely. |
| Blood donation (acute) | No clear reduction | Weak | Systematic review of whole-blood donation (~500 mL): the change in VO2 max was **not statistically significant**, and the review concludes VO2 max is not reduced by donation. Submaximal responses do shift. The stroke-volume / cardiac-output mechanism is absent from the paper. | Losing ~500 mL of blood sounds like it should dent your VO2 max, but the pooled evidence does not show a significant drop. The review is small and individual studies disagree, so do not plan around a large effect either way. |
| Dehydration / hypohydration | Negative | Moderate | Losing more than ~2% of body mass as water lowers maximal aerobic capacity, mainly by shrinking plasma volume, which reduces stroke volume and cardiac output. The impairment compounds in the heat (high skin temperature). A narrative review rather than a pooled analysis, hence Moderate; do not attach a per-degree percentage figure to it. | Showing up dehydrated lowers your VO2 max on the day because there is less blood volume to pump. Hot conditions make it worse. Hydrate before testing or hard efforts. |
| Genetics (trainability) | Variable | Strong | HERITAGE Family Study (n=481, 98 families, 20 wk identical training): mean gain ~400 mL/min but ranged from no gain to >1.0 L/min. ~47% was the *maximal* (upper-bound) heritability estimate for the response; 2.5x more variance between families than within. Caucasian family sample. | How much your VO2 max improves from the same training is heavily genetic — up to roughly half of the response. Some people are high responders, some barely move. It runs in families. |
| Sex | Variable | Moderate (narrative review) | ~10 to 12% endurance performance gap. Comparing elite men with elite women, male VO2 max averages ~10% higher **per kg of body mass**; the review gives no per-fat-free-mass figure. Driven mainly by greater oxygen delivery (higher hemoglobin mass, larger heart). | Men average higher VO2 max largely because they carry more oxygen per unit of blood and have bigger hearts. The ~10% figure is elite-versus-elite, per kilogram of body weight. |

*Anchors: Fleg et al. 2005 (age); Bouchard et al. 1999 HERITAGE (genetics); Santisteban et al. 2022 (sex); Saltin et al. 1968 Dallas Bed Rest (immobilization); van Baak et al. 2021 (body fat); Johnson et al. 2019 (blood donation); Cheuvront/Sawka 2010 (dehydration).*

## Lifestyle

| Factor | Direction | Evidence | Key Finding | Plain English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smoking | Negative | Moderate | Cross-sectional (n=70, step-test estimate): current smokers had lower estimated VO2 max (38.8 mL/kg/min) than never-smokers (41.6) and former smokers (41.4); former and never smokers did not differ. The carbon-monoxide-binds-hemoglobin mechanism is standard physiology, not measured here. The authors disclose tobacco-industry-funded research ties. | Smokers in this study had a measurably lower aerobic ceiling. Because it is a single cross-sectional snapshot, it cannot show that quitting restores it. |
| Alcohol (previous-day) | Negative (performance) | Moderate | RCT (n=12, Shaw et al. 2022 — first author is Shaw, not Levitt): a previous-evening dose of 1.09 g ethanol per kg fat-free mass cut next-morning cycling time to exhaustion by ~11% (181 vs 203 s); strength tests were unaffected. Effect is on endurance performance/capacity rather than a proven change in VO2 max itself. | Drinking the night before a hard session measurably hurts endurance the next day. The hit is to how long you can hold an effort, more than to your VO2 max ceiling. |
| Sleep deprivation | Negative (performance) | Moderate | Meta-analysis of 45 studies, **16 of them on aerobic endurance**: sleep deprivation cut aerobic endurance performance (SMD -0.76, 95% CI -1.27 to -0.25; athletes -0.66, non-athletes -1.02) and raised perceived exertion. The paper reports **no VO2 max outcome at all** — both the "acute loss spares VO2 max" and "chronic short sleep lowers VO2 max" claims are unsourced here and must not be attributed to it. | A bad night clearly hurts how long and how hard you can go. What it does to your VO2 max number specifically has not been tested here. |
| Sauna bathing (habitual) | Positive | Moderate | Kuopio (KIHD) cohort of **2,012 men** aged 42 to 61. Versus one session a week: 2-3 sessions/week +0.84 mL/kg/min (p=.008), 4-7 sessions/week +1.17 (p=.041); at 11 years only 2-3/week stayed significant (+1.22, p=.038). **Duration** of a sauna session was not associated with CRF. The ~+2.7 mL/kg/min post-exercise-sauna RCT is by the same group, not independent corroboration. | More frequent sauna sessions track with higher fitness in Finnish men; how long you sit does not. Observational, and the one supporting trial is from the same authors. |
| Sedentary behavior (sitting time) | Negative | Moderate | In 16,025 adults, more daily sitting was associated with lower **estimated** cardiorespiratory fitness — but **no association was found among the highly active**, so it is not independent of exercise. Fitness was estimated, not directly measured. | Long sitting days track with lower fitness, mainly in people who are not otherwise very active. If you already train a lot, this study does not show sitting hurting you. |
| Cold-water immersion (post-training) | No VO2 max estimate | Moderate | Meta-analysis: routinely ice-bathing after sessions blunts strength and power gains (SMD -0.60). **No pooled VO2 max estimate exists in this review**, so it neither confirms nor rules out an effect on aerobic capacity — and the "some studies suggest attenuation" caveat could not be verified against it. | Ice baths clearly cost you strength gains. For VO2 max there is no pooled evidence either way. |

*Anchors: Caci et al. 2024 (smoking; step-test estimated VO2 max, small sample, industry-funded ties disclosed); Shaw et al. 2022 (alcohol; n=12, time-to-exhaustion not VO2 max); Kong et al. 2025 meta (sleep deprivation; no VO2 max outcome); Kunutsor et al. 2024 (sauna; observational + same-group RCT); Eriksen et al. 2016 (sedentary behavior; observational, n=16,025, estimated CRF); Malta et al. 2021 meta (cold-water immersion; no pooled VO2 max estimate).*

## Clinical

| Factor | Direction | Evidence | Key Finding | Plain English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long COVID | Negative | Moderate | Meta-analysis (9 studies, 464 symptomatic vs 359): peak VO2 was 4.9 mL/kg/min lower in people with ongoing symptoms 3+ months after infection, at **low certainty of evidence**. Included cohorts **oversampled people who had been hospitalized**. Drivers include deconditioning, dysfunctional breathing, chronotropic incompetence, and impaired oxygen extraction. | Lingering COVID symptoms come with a measurably lower VO2 max. Certainty is low and the studies lean toward people who were hospitalized, so milder cases may be less affected. |
| Type 2 diabetes | Negative | Moderate | Meta-analysis (77 studies): people with type 2 diabetes averaged 5.84 mL/kg/min lower VO2 max than those without. Driven by cardiac, microvascular, and mitochondrial impairments. (The "roughly 20% lower" restatement is not supported by the paper.) | Type 2 diabetes meaningfully lowers aerobic fitness, and low fitness in turn predicts worse outcomes. Exercise that raises VO2 max is one of the highest-value interventions here. |
| Heart failure (and VO2 max as prognosis) | Negative | Strong | Heart failure lowers peak VO2, and the value itself is prognostic: in the landmark CPET study, peak VO2 ≤14 mL/kg/min became the heart-transplant timing threshold — but that group still had ~70% one-year survival. The **worst** prognosis belonged to patients rejected from transplant for noncardiac reasons (47% one-year, 32% two-year survival). | The heart can't deliver enough oxygen, so VO2 max falls and is one of the strongest predictors of survival in heart failure. The classic ≤14 figure is a transplant-timing threshold, not the worst-prognosis group in the original data. |
| Low fitness as a mortality risk factor | Predictor (higher = lower mortality) | Strong | In 122,007 adults followed ~8.4 years, higher **treadmill MET-derived cardiorespiratory fitness — not laboratory VO2 max** — was associated with lower all-cause mortality with no upper limit of benefit; the least-fit had ~5x the adjusted mortality risk of the fittest, comparable to or greater than smoking, diabetes, or coronary disease. Observational: an association, not proof that raising fitness lowers risk. | The "why it matters" headline: low fitness is one of the strongest markers of dying early, on par with major disease risk factors — and there's no point of diminishing returns. This is exercise-test fitness in METs rather than a lab VO2 max. |

*Anchors: Durstenfeld et al. 2022 (long COVID; low certainty, hospitalized-skewed); Macedo et al. 2023 meta (type 2 diabetes); Mancini et al. 1991 (heart failure prognosis / transplant threshold); Mandsager et al. 2018 (MET-derived CRF and all-cause mortality, n=122,007).*

## Wearable VO2 Max Estimation: Methods & Accuracy

Important framing for Kygo's audience: consumer wearables do not *measure* VO2 max. The lab gold standard (CPET / indirect calorimetry) analyzes the oxygen and carbon dioxide in your breath during a maximal test. Watches and straps have no gas analysis, so they *estimate* VO2 max from heart rate, motion, GPS pace, and your profile (age, sex, weight). Treat the number as a fitness trend tracker, not a clinical value. This section complements files #06 (Wearable Accuracy) and #07 (Sensor Comparison).

The single most useful accuracy finding comes from the INTERLIVE meta-analysis of consumer wearables: **how the device collects the data matters more than the brand.** Wearables that estimate VO2 max from an actual workout (exercise-based) had near-zero average bias vs lab (−0.09 mL/kg/min), while those that estimate it at rest (resting-based) overestimated by +2.17 mL/kg/min. Both, however, have large *individual* error (limits of agreement roughly ±13 to 17 mL/kg/min for resting methods), which is why the number is a trend/population tool, not a precise personal value.

Two estimation approaches:

- **Exercise-based HR-to-pace modeling (more accurate).** During an outdoor walk/run the device models the heart-rate-to-pace relationship and extrapolates to your max. Used by Garmin (Firstbeat), Apple (outdoor walk/run/hike), Polar (running-based test), Samsung (outdoor run), Coros/Suunto/Huawei (similar HR-to-pace), and WHOOP's GPS model. Needs a real cardio effort, a clean HR signal, and a correct max-HR assumption.
- **Resting / non-exercise estimation (least accurate, tends to overestimate).** From resting heart rate plus age/sex/weight, no workout needed. Used by Polar's Fitness Test (resting HR + HRV), Fitbit's cardio fitness score without GPS, and WHOOP's passive model. Oura's Cardio Capacity sits in between: a guided in-app 6-minute walk test.

The table below separates what each company *claims* from what *independent* (non-vendor) studies found vs lab CPET. Pattern: vendors quote best-case numbers; independent studies are a bit worse and consistently show errors balloon in highly trained athletes and with wrist-only optical HR.

**Verification note (triple-checked).** Independent (non-vendor) peer-reviewed VO2 max validation exists only for Garmin (Carrier 2025; Engel 2025; plus the Garmin-heavy Železnik Mežan 2025 review and INTERLIVE meta), Apple (Lambe 2025; Caserman 2024), Polar (Neudorfer 2025), and Fitbit (Freeberg 2019). For Samsung, WHOOP, Oura, and Coros, repeated targeted searches found no independent VO2 max validation — only vendor or company-funded claims. A frequently-repeated "Frontiers in Physiology, Galaxy Watch 5/6, 84 participants, ±4.7 mL/kg/min" VO2 max figure appears only on AI-content/marketing sites and could not be traced to a real indexed study (PubMed/PMC/journal); it is deliberately excluded. If any of these brands publish independent validation later, add it here.

Bottom line: a VO2 max estimate from a hard outdoor run with a chest strap (Garmin/Firstbeat, the only brand with solid independent validation — ~5 to 7% error in general populations) is the most trustworthy. Apple has two independent studies and both show it *underestimates* (~13 to 16%). Polar's resting test and Fitbit overestimate. Samsung, WHOOP, Oura, and Coros rely on company claims with little or no independent peer-reviewed VO2 max validation. For every device, accuracy degrades in highly trained people (errors of 9 to 16%), with wrist-only optical HR, in heat, and when the assumed max HR is wrong. Use the number to track *your own trend over time*, not to compare people or hit a clinical threshold.

*Anchors (independent): Molina-García et al. 2022 INTERLIVE meta (exercise- vs resting-based); Carrier et al. 2025 + Engel et al. 2025 + Železnik Mežan 2025 (Garmin); Lambe et al. 2025 + Caserman et al. 2024 (Apple); Neudorfer et al. 2025 (Polar); Freeberg et al. 2019 (Fitbit). Vendor/company-associated claims (Firstbeat, Samsung/U. Michigan, WHOOP, Oura, Coros) are labeled as such and not independently verified.*

VO2 max is only as accurate as the signals fed into it. None of these devices measure oxygen; they infer VO2 max from heart rate, GPS pace, and your profile. Table A shows what goes in; Table B shows how well-validated each input is independently. The punchline: the limiting input is heart rate *during the run* plus the assumed maximum HR, which is exactly why a chest strap helps and why very fit people get poor readings.

| Input signal | How good it is (independent evidence) | Source |
|---|---|---|

**Reconciliation with #06/#07 (checked).** The heart-rate and sensor figures above were sourced fresh and then cross-checked against file #06 (Wearable Accuracy). They agree: #06 independently lists chest strap r=0.99, Apple active-HR r=0.80, Garmin r=0.52 (Dial et al. 2025), Oura resting-HR CCC 0.98, and — importantly — the same VO2 max figures used here (Garmin Fēnix 6 MAPE 7.05%, Apple 13-16%). No contradictions were found between this section and #06/#07. The one caveat carried over from the library's Oura file: Oura's headline HR/HRV validation (Kinnunen 2020) is Oura-employee-authored; independent work (Cao 2022, Dial 2025) confirms strong *resting* accuracy but Oura has no active-exercise HR.

Why this matters for VO2 max: a device can have a good HR sensor and still produce a shaky VO2 max, because the estimate leans on HR *during hard effort* (where wrist optical drifts) and on an assumed max HR (often just 220−age). That combination — not the sensor at rest — is the real ceiling on accuracy, and it explains why chest-strap + outdoor run (Garmin) is the most trustworthy setup and why highly trained users see the biggest errors.

### Batch 10 (added 2026-06-02) — Why it matters

48. **Clinical / mortality.** Mandsager K, Harb S, Cremer P, Phelan D, Nissen SE, Jaber W. 52. **Environment / hypoxic training (contradicting evidence).** Dorelli G, et al. "Aerobic Intermittent Hypoxic Training Is Not Beneficial for Maximal Oxygen Uptake and Performance: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports.* 2025;35:e70088. PMID [40548904](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40548904/). DOI [10.1111/sms.70088](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70088).
   - Design: systematic review + meta-analysis, 35 studies, 524 participants, parallel-design aerobic IHT vs normoxic training. Verified findings: no significant improvement in VO2max (p=0.333), peak power output (p=0.159), or time to exhaustion (p=0.410); no subgroup differences by fitness level or exercise modality; publication bias detected (p=0.004). This is the paper source 33's note previously referred to only as "a 2025 meta" — and it **contradicts** the IHT portion of the Feng 2023 ranking rather than complementing it.
