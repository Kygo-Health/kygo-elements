Moderate

Higher carb diet

About 9 min more REM

Moderate

High carb meals or diets gave about 9 min more REM vs low carb. Low carb shifted sleep toward deep sleep instead. Not every study agrees: one small cyclist trial and one observational study tied protein or fat, not carbs, to more REM.

FAQ

Common questions.

How does food affect your sleep?

Mostly one stage at a time. Caffeine costs deep sleep (about 11 minutes on average across 24 studies), slows falling asleep by about 9 minutes and adds about 12 minutes awake, but leaves REM unchanged. Alcohol costs REM (it starts about 18 minutes later and runs about 11 minutes shorter) without adding any deep sleep overall. Carbohydrate tilts the night toward REM, while lower carb eating tilts it toward deep sleep. Many popular claims, such as almonds, a late dinner on a single night, or a Mediterranean diet on a sleep tracker, showed no measured change.

What foods help you get more deep sleep?

The best-tested food effect on deep sleep is a negative one: caffeine cuts it by about 11 minutes on average, and 400mg (about 4 cups of coffee) still cut it by 20.6 minutes when taken 12 hours before bed, while 100mg had no measurable effect. On the positive side, lower carb eating gave about 8 minutes more deep sleep in a meta-analysis of 11 small studies, and in lab studies a higher fiber, lower saturated fat day predicted more deep sleep that night, though that is an observational link. A very low carb (keto) diet raised deep sleep from about 14% to 18% of the night in one small, non-randomized study.

What foods increase REM sleep?

Higher carb meals and diets gave about 9 minutes more REM than low carb in a meta-analysis of 11 small studies. Not every study agrees, but a controlled trial found a high protein, lower carb diet cut REM by about 9 minutes and pushed the first REM period about 15 minutes later. The biggest REM effects run the other way: alcohol delays and shortens REM from around 2 to 3 drinks, and daytime fasting dropped REM from 25.6% to 18.6% of sleep. Caffeine, a late dinner and almonds did not change REM.

Does eating late at night affect sleep?

The controlled evidence is split. In a randomized lab study, eating dinner at 10pm instead of 6pm for one night did not change REM, deep sleep, time awake or sleep efficiency (Gu 2020, n=20). Over 4 days, eating 1 hour instead of 5 hours before bed raised time awake from 16 to 29 minutes and cut about 26 minutes of sleep (Enomoto 2026, n=13, funded by a food company). A survey of 124,239 people linked eating within an hour of bed to 2 to 2.6x the odds of 30+ minutes awake, but those people also slept 25 to 35 minutes longer, so it may reflect who eats late rather than the food.

How long before bed should you stop caffeine?

It depends heavily on dose. In a double-blind trial, 400mg of caffeine (about 4 standard cups of coffee) cut deep sleep by 29.7 minutes when taken 4 hours before bed and still cut it by 20.6 minutes when taken 12 hours before bed. 100mg had no measurable effect at any timing. Across studies, caffeine adds about 9 minutes to falling asleep, and in that pooled data dose and timing did not change the effect.

Does alcohol help you sleep?

It helps you fall asleep only at a high dose, about 5 or more drinks, while REM is disrupted from about 2 drinks: REM starts about 18 minutes later and runs about 11 minutes shorter on average across 27 studies. At about 5 drinks, deep sleep rises in the first half of the night and falls in the second half, so the total does not go up, and time awake rises. Pooled lab studies found no clear change in total time awake across the night.

Does tart cherry juice help you sleep?

One crossover trial in 15 older adults with insomnia found about 17 minutes less time awake on tart cherry juice than placebo (62 vs 79 minutes), but it was funded entirely by the juice maker and used a sleep diary. Later trials are mixed: a 2024 capsule trial using an EEG headband and Fitbit, and a 2022 diary trial in 44 healthy adults, found nothing, while a small trial in older adults with insomnia and a 2012 actigraphy trial found more sleep.

Will my wearable show these effects?

Some of them. Effects on time to fall asleep, time awake and stage minutes are what a ring or watch estimates, but most of the changes here are 5 to 15 minutes, which is within a wearable's night-to-night noise, so test a food over several nights, not one. Some changes a wearable cannot see at all: a week of junk food made deep sleep shallower on EEG without changing deep sleep minutes. Kygo lines up your logged meals, caffeine and alcohol with your own sleep data across many nights, which is the practical way to see whether a food matters for you.
