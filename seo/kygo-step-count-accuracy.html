MAPE is the average percentage a count is off, so lower is better. Lab is a treadmill or structured walk; free-living is a normal day against a research-grade reference. Figures are pooled across the models actually studied, which may not be yours.

A lab number is not a day number. Around 5% in controlled walking, above 10% in real life, on every device here.

Model beats brand. Fitbit spans 3.6% to 35.5%; Garmin spans 1% to 15% low. A brand name without a model number tells you nothing.

No data is not good data. Tier 4 means nobody independent has measured them, not that they failed. It cuts both ways: COROS moved up a tier the moment someone tested it.

Nothing on sale today has been validated for steps. The newest device with published data is about four years old: everything from 2025 on, Apple Series 11 through Oura Ring 5 and Pixel Watch 4, is untested. Two models people ask about do not exist at all: no Fitbit Charge 7, no Polar Vantage V4.

What actually decides it

It is your speed and your arms, not the brand.

A wrist tracker does not detect footfalls. It detects arm movement that looks like walking, and everything else follows from that.

Normal arm swing, normal pace

Under 5% error

0.9 to 1.8 m/s, arms free

Walk at a normal or brisk pace with your arms swinging and every device on this page is usable. This is also the condition almost every published accuracy figure was measured in, which is why the marketing sounds so good.

Arms occupied, or a slow pace

35 to 95% of steps missed

Stroller, cart, pockets, handrail

Push a pram or a shopping trolley, carry bags, hold a treadmill handrail or walk with your hands in your pockets and the arm swing disappears. The steps still happen; the watch just does not see them. Slow walking does the same thing for a different reason.

It runs both ways. Gesturing, cooking and road vibration while driving all hit roughly walking cadence, so they add steps you never took. Garmin draws the fewest of these complaints; a finger-worn ring the most. Roos 2020 · Kristiansson 2023 · Kim 2024

The two things that decide it

How fast you walk, and where you wear it.

These two move your step count more than any brand choice. Both are things you control, and neither appears on a spec sheet.

True for every device

What no brand can fix.

Four things no tracker can fix, because they are about your gait and your hands rather than the sensor.

Walking speed

Below 0.9 m/s, everything fails

Above about 0.9 m/s every device here is usable. Below it accuracy collapses, and under 0.5 m/s even the best miss most of your steps. Anyone recovering from surgery or using a cane spends their day well outside the conditions the published figures were measured in.

Roos 2020 · Feehan 2020 · Johnston 2021

Mobility aids

A rolling walker costs a third of your steps

The largest single error on this page. Across 42 adults aged 51 to 80, an Apple Watch Series 8 undercounted by 36.4% with a rolling walker. Your arm is resting on the frame, not swinging. A waist monitor helps but still misses at very slow speeds.

Özel 2026 (N=42, manual reference)

Gait pathology

Clinical gait is a different problem

Step algorithms are trained on healthy walking. In neurological conditions such as stroke or Parkinson's they detect only 11 to 47% of steps, so a consumer wearable will understate rehabilitation progress, sometimes by more than half.

Sensors 2025 · Johnston 2021

Age and gait

Your gait changes the number

Apple Watch averaged 4.3% error under 40 and 10.9% at 40 and over. Age is not the mechanism: shorter steps, slower speed and reduced arm swing are. Body composition shows the same indirect effect through gait rather than through BMI.

Choe & Kang 2025 · Modave 2017 · Scataglini 2025

Claim vs reality

What gets quoted, and what holds up.

Where the headline figures actually come from, including one this page used to quote itself.

The "82.6% accurate" ranking

“Garmin 82.6%, Apple Watch 81.1%, Fitbit 77.3% overall step accuracy.”

Quoted everywhere, including by an earlier version of this page. It comes from a consumer data aggregation, not a study, and no peer-reviewed paper reports an "overall accuracy percentage" for a step counter. The real measure is MAPE, always per model and per condition.

WellnessPulse 2025 (consumer aggregate, not peer reviewed)

Oura: "Real Steps"

“A new algorithm better distinguishes walking from hand movement.”

Plausible, and counts did fall about 20% after the March 2025 rollout. But a lower number is not a validated number. We searched PubMed, Google Scholar, DOAJ and OpenAIRE, plus a 138-study smart-ring scoping review: nothing validates Real Steps, Ring 4 or Ring 5 for step counting. Every Oura step figure on this page comes from a Gen 2 or Gen 3 ring.

Oura Health 2025 (manufacturer). Literature searched August 2026, no validation found

Google: the enhanced step algorithm

“Wear OS 5.1 improves step detection for strollers, carts and hiking poles.”

It inflated counts on every Pixel Watch and was reverted a month later. A second inflation bug hit in March 2026 and the history was never corrected. The same watch on the same wrist counted differently in three consecutive months.

Google Wear OS release notes (manufacturer)

Polar: "overreports by 3.8%"

“A Vantage M3 logged 10,479 steps against 10,102 and 10,142 on two phones.”

There is no criterion measure in it. A phone in your pocket is not ground truth. The one hand-tally study has a Polar 17% low, the opposite direction. Polar is contested, not simply overreporting.

TechRadar (no criterion) vs Rider 2025 and Henriksen 2022

Garmin: "a 10-step minimum bout filter"

“Garmin records nothing until 10 consecutive steps, then credits all 10.”

Not in any Garmin manual or support document. It traces to a user post on Garmin's community forum from around 2015. The behaviour is real, Garmin does draw the fewest phantom-step complaints, but the mechanism is folklore.

Garmin Venu 4 Owner's Manual

The test that catches all of these. If a source gives an accuracy percentage without naming a criterion measure and an error metric, it is not an accuracy figure.

Beyond the step count

An accurate step count is not an accurate anything else.

Steps, active minutes and calories come off the same sensor on the same wrist, and they are nowhere near equally trustworthy.

The step count was almost perfect. Everything built on top of it was not. Against a research-grade ActiGraph, the same watch over the same days read steps within about 2%, undercounted moderate-to-vigorous activity by nearly half, and overcounted energy expenditure by a quarter. In the same study the Fitbit Sense overcounted steps by 18.0%.

Do not read a good step number as a good day. A footfall is a countable event; active minutes and calories are inferred from intensity, and the inference is where the error lives. Miwa 2026, funded by Chugai Pharmaceutical (authors are employees and shareholders).

The bottom line

If the step total matters to you, wear a Garmin or an Apple Watch, with Samsung third. Fitbit reads about 15% over in daily life. COROS is the surprise, beating both in the one recent hand-tally test. Do not buy an Oura Ring for steps, or judge it by them. Polar is contested, and nobody has measured the Pixel Watch or WHOOP at all.

But no purchase fixes the real limits: nothing is accurate below 0.9 m/s, a walker costs you a third of your steps, where you wear it matters more than what you buy, and an accurate step count still buys you a calorie number that is 26% out. Use it as a trend against your own yesterday, not a measurement against someone else's watch.

FAQ

Common questions.

Which wearable has the most accurate step count?

Garmin, then Apple Watch, then Samsung. Pooled across 59 free-living studies, Garmin read about 3% over and Apple Watch about 7% over (Paluch 2026). Samsung is third on smaller studies: the Galaxy Watch 4 had 10.5% error over 24 hours, and the Galaxy Watch Active 2 counted 7.2% low on slow walks. Fitbit does not place, reading about 15% over in the same pooled data. The one recent hand-tally head-to-head went to COROS, but it was a single 12-person study.

How accurate is the Apple Watch step counter?

Better than most, and unusually consistent. Over 24 hours against an ActivPAL thigh sensor the Series 6 hit 6.4% error at r=0.99. Two caveats: slow or light walking pushed error to 23.9%, and adults 40 and over averaged 10.9% against 4.3% for under 40. The age effect is really a gait effect.

Is the Oura Ring accurate for step counting?

No, and this is the one device where the research agrees with itself. Every study that has measured Oura steps found it overcounting: +2,124 a day over 14 days against a pedometer, +1,416 a day over a week against a research accelerometer, and a third putting agreement at r=0.77 and concluding Oura cannot replace an ActiGraph for steps. Two different reference devices, same direction, similar size. A finger sensor reads hand movement, so cooking, typing and gesturing all register as walking. Use the ring for sleep and HRV.

What is the biggest factor affecting step count accuracy?

Walking speed, by a distance. Above about 0.9 m/s every device here is usable. Below it accuracy collapses on all of them, and under 0.5 m/s even the best miss most of your steps. Published figures are almost always measured at normal or brisk speeds, while older adults and anyone using a walker routinely walk slower than that.

Does Garmin overcount or undercount steps?

In daily life it slightly overcounts: about 3% over pooled across 59 free-living studies (Paluch 2026), the closest of any brand. It undercounts at slow speeds, and one hand-tally study had the Fenix 6 15% low over mixed activity. Garmin also draws the fewest phantom-step complaints. Be careful with the usual explanation though: the "10-step minimum bout" mechanic is not in any Garmin document, it traces to a user forum post.

Why does my wearable count steps when I am driving or sitting?

Because a wrist device detects arm motion that looks like walking, not footfalls. Road vibration, desk work, brushing your teeth and talking with your hands all match the cadence pattern the algorithm watches for. Garmin says as much in its own manual. Samsung and Oura draw the most reports; Garmin the fewest.

Are step counters accurate if I use a walker, cane or stroller?

No, and this is the largest single error on the page. With a rolling walker an Apple Watch Series 8 undercounted by 36.4% against a manual count, and slow walking cost a further 16.3%. Your arm is resting on the frame instead of swinging, so the watch has nothing to read. A waist-worn monitor helps but still underestimates at very slow speeds.

If my step count is accurate, are my calories accurate too?

No, and the gap is bigger than most people expect. Against a research-grade ActiGraph, the same Apple Watch read steps within 2% but undercounted active minutes by 46% and overcounted calories by 26%. Steps are countable events; active minutes and calories are inferred from intensity, and that is where the error compounds.
