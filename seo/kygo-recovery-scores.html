HRV falls with age, so a "low" number for a 60-year-old can be perfectly normal. Compare to age norms and your own baseline, never to other people.

Effect on the signals
HRV declines with age (~80 ms in teens to ~25 ms over 75).
Evidence
Strong
Mechanism
Vagal tone and autonomic flexibility decline with age.
What to do
Judge your score against your own history and age-appropriate norms.

Source

Age, sex & fitness differences in HRV (PMC6686273)

Body fat percentage — not weight or BMI — is the informative measure: higher body fat tracks lower HRV and higher RHR, and it improves with fat loss.

Effect on the signals
Higher body fat tracks lower HRV and higher RHR; BMI alone is not independently associated.
Evidence
Moderate
Mechanism
Adipose tissue raises sympathetic tone and inflammatory load; fat loss restores autonomic balance.
What to do
Track body fat %, not the scale; sustainable fat loss lifts the baseline.

Body fat percentage & HRV sympathovagal balance (PMC3743431)

Normal physiology, not a problem: expect lower scores mid-to-late cycle as RHR and temperature rise and HRV dips.

Effect on the signals
RHR +~3–5 bpm (~3%), HRV down, temperature +~0.5°C in the luteal phase.
Evidence
Moderate–strong
Mechanism
Progesterone raises basal temperature and heart rate and lowers vagal tone in the luteal phase.
What to do
Expect and accept lower numbers in the luteal phase; compare within-cycle, not across phases.

Menstrual cycle & cardiac autonomic function / HRV (PMC5588411)

Women tend to run higher vagal tone, men higher sympathetic — and the gap narrows after about 55 (menopause).

Effect on the signals
Women: higher vagal tone; men: higher sympathetic. The gap narrows after ~55 (menopause).
Evidence
Moderate
Mechanism
Sex hormones modulate autonomic balance; the difference attenuates post-menopause.

Apple Watch

Readiness · 0–10 · Series 12 and Ultra 4 only · free

Score name
Readiness · Free
How it's built
A 0–10 score launched in September 2026, built from three groups of inputs compared with your own recent baseline: Activity (active calories, workout effort ratings and Training Load), Vitals (overnight heart rate, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, HRV and blood oxygen, plus daytime vitals) and last night's Sleep Score. The keystone paper

Doherty, Baldwin, Altini et al., "Readiness, recovery, and strain: an evaluation of composite health scores in consumer wearables" (Translational Exercise Biomedicine, De Gruyter, 2025) catalogued 14 composite scores across 10 manufacturers. Co-author Marco Altini is an HRV scientist and an Oura advisor, so this is not an anti-wearable piece. Verbatim: "None of the manufacturers disclosed their exact algorithmic formulas, and few provided empirical validation or peer-reviewed evidence supporting the accuracy or clinical relevance of their scores." Most common inputs: HRV 86%, RHR 79%, activity 71%, sleep 71%.

Eight headlines from the data

If you only remember eight things.

What you're buying

One number

A modeled opinion, not a measurement

Almost every brand now sells a single morning number for "how recovered are you." But there's no clinical gold standard for recovery the way polysomnography exists for sleep — so brands validate their inputs, not the output. A recovery score is a modeled opinion layered on real signals.

Cross-brand comparison

Don't

An "80" means different things

Different inputs, baselines, and scales. Polar isn't even on 0–100 (it's ANS Charge, −10 to +10). Two devices on one wrist will routinely disagree. Only the trend versus your own baseline is meaningful — never the cross-brand number.

Scores independently validated

2 / 12

Only WHOOP & Oura

And WHOOP's came out weak — the raw HRV carried the signal, the composite didn't add to it. Everyone else has at most component validation or vendor white papers. "2 of 12" is the headline finding, not a coverage gap.

The common spine

HRV+RHR+sleep

Same ingredients, different recipe

Across the De Gruyter catalogue: HRV in 86% of scores, RHR 79%, activity 71%, sleep 71%. The differences between brands are weighting and window length, not the raw ingredients.

Biggest controllable hit

~15%

Alcohol drops HRV overnight

Oura's 600,000-member data: HRV down ~15% (~10.8 ms), HR up ~9.6%. WHOOP: a single drink cuts next-day recovery ~8%. Dose-dependent, and the clearest lever a food-logging user can actually see and change.

Single biggest lever up

Sleep

Consistency = duration

Adequate, regular sleep is the dominant positive input across every brand. Regularity matters as much as hours. Everything else — supplements, cold plunges, breathwork — is a rounding error next to it.

Signals beat scores

Oura best

Cite the signal, not the score

Dial 2025 (536 nights vs ECG): Oura HRV CCC 0.97–0.99, WHOOP 0.94, Garmin and Polar poor. The ingredients are reasonably sound (best in Oura); the recipe layered on top is the unvalidated part.

The independence problem

Double-count

One bad night hits 3 inputs

Poor sleep lowers next-day HRV and raises RHR — so a score "combining" sleep + HRV + RHR partly counts the same event twice. This can overstate a single bad night, a known critique of every composite score.

Common questions

Recovery scores, answered.

Can I compare a recovery score across different brands?

No. Every brand uses different inputs, baselines, and scales, so a "75" on one device does not mean a "75" on another. Polar is not even on a 0–100 scale (ANS Charge runs −10 to +10). Two devices on the same wrist will routinely disagree. Only the trend versus your own personal baseline is meaningful — never the cross-brand number.

Are wearable recovery scores actually validated?

Mostly no. Of 12 brands, only WHOOP and Oura have ever been tested against an external reference, and WHOOP's came out weak — the raw HRV carried the signal while the composite Recovery score added no consistent association. Oura has moderate support. Everyone else has at most component validation or vendor white papers, not an independent test of the score itself. There is no clinical gold standard for "recovery" the way polysomnography is for sleep, so brands validate their inputs, not the output. A recovery score is a modeled opinion, not a measurement.

Which brands have a free recovery score?

Free: Garmin Training Readiness, Fitbit/Pixel Daily Readiness (free since app v4.27 — most articles still wrongly say "Premium required"), Samsung Energy Score, Polar Nightly Recharge, Ultrahuman Dynamic Recovery, Coros, Amazfit HybridCharge, Suunto Body Resources, and RingConn. Paid: WHOOP (band requires a membership) and Oura (membership ~$5.99/mo). Apple Watch Readiness is free with no subscription stated, but only on Series 12 and Ultra 4; older Apple Watches still need a third-party app.

What lowers your recovery score the most?

Alcohol is the biggest single controllable hit — Oura's 600,000-member data shows HRV down about 15% (~10.8 ms) and heart rate up about 9.6%, and WHOOP found a single drink cuts next-day recovery roughly 8%. Sleep loss, hard training or overreaching, and the onset of illness are the other strong-evidence drops. Late or high-glycemic meals near bed, evening caffeine, dehydration, altitude, and nicotine are moderate hits.

Does the Apple Watch have a recovery score?

Yes, since September 2026, but only on Apple Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4. Apple's Readiness score runs from 0 to 10 (Recover, Pace Yourself, Ready, Go For It) and combines your recent activity and Training Load, your overnight and daytime vitals, and your Sleep Score, all against your own baseline. It updates through the day and needs at least 7 nights of sleep data before it appears. Apple has published no validation of the score. Older Apple Watches still rely on third-party apps like Athlytic, Bevel, Gentler Streak and Training Today.

Which supplements actually help recovery?

The defensible ones, by evidence: magnesium (bisglycinate), glycine, tart cherry, L-theanine, ashwagandha, and saffron. Omega-3 (DHA) modestly lowers resting heart rate. Melatonin is a circadian timing aid, not a recovery booster. Do not rely on the trendy isolated apigenin (it has no human sleep RCT — the evidence is for whole chamomile extract), or on valerian, GABA, CBD, or creatine as recovery aids. Trial quality across this whole category is often low; doses are study doses, not recommendations, and this is not medical advice.

Why do my two wearables show different recovery numbers?

Because they are not measuring the same thing the same way. The common spine is HRV + resting heart rate + sleep differenced against a personal baseline, but each brand weights those differently, uses a different window (Polar reads only the first ~4 hours of sleep; Garmin and Coros lean on training load), and anchors to a different baseline length. The scores are inferences, not measurements, so disagreement is expected.

How does what I eat and drink affect my recovery score?

Alcohol, late or heavy meals within ~3 hours of bed, high-glycemic spikes near bedtime, evening caffeine, and dehydration all move the HRV + RHR + sleep signals your score is built on. These are the levers a food-logging user can actually see and change, and the brands' own blogs tend to underplay them — which is exactly why connecting nutrition to your recovery trend is the highest-value angle.
